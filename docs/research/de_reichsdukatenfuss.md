# The imperial ducat standard — ordinances and parameters, 1340-1876

**Subject.** The German-side legal history of the gold standard this project
files as `reichsdukatenfuss`: which act set which figure, when, and what replaced
it. The emphasis throughout is on **instruments and parameters**, not on coins.
Individual pieces appear only where a first strike fixes the date an issuer
entered or left the standard.

**Deliberately out of scope.**

- **Where the standard's figures come from** — the Florentine descent, the
  Hungarian and Venetian arithmetic, why 67 and not 66: **`ducat_origins.md`**,
  shared with the Danish side. This dossier begins where that one ends and takes
  its conclusions as given.
- **Denmark-Norway's own coinage on the standard**:
  `dk_dukat_portugaloeser_fod.md`.
- **The Rhenish Goldgulden as a coinage in its own right.** It appears in §1
  because its ordinance line is the ducat's legal prehistory and because the
  ducat displaced it, not for itself.
- **The Vereinsmünzfuß and Reichsgoldmünzfuß** that succeed it: `vereinsgoldmuenze.md`,
  `krone_muentzfuesse.md`.

## 1. The legal prehistory: the Rhenish gulden's ordinance line, 1340-1490

The ducat did not arrive into a vacuum. For a century and a half the Reich's
gold coin was the Rheinischer Goldgulden, and its standard was set — and then
repeatedly lowered — by a documented chain of acts. That chain is what the ducat
eventually replaced, and its shape explains why a second name was needed.

| year | instrument | fineness | Schrot |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1340** | Ludwig IV grants **Lübeck** the right to strike gold — the first German city to receive one; first Goldgulden 1341 | — | — |
| before 1365 | individual Rhenish electors strike Florene at Deutz, Oberwesel, Heidelberg, Bacharach, Eltville, Bingen | **23⅔ Karat** | «3,5 g schwer» |
| 1372 | Trier + Cologne + the city of Cologne; the gulden set at 18½ Weißpfennige | (Deutz coins taken as pattern) | — |
| **26 Nov 1385** | the prince-archbishops of Mainz, Trier and Cologne with the Count Palatine — the **Rheinischer Münzverein**, first common regulation | 23 Karat | — |
| **1386** | the modified treaty | **23 Karat (958/1000)** | **66 pieces to the Cologne Mark of 233,856 g**, 3,54 g |
| 1399 | the union renewal | 22½ Karat | — |
| **1402** | **Reichsgesetz of King Ruprecht** — an attempt to make the gulden a stable imperial coin | 22½ Karat | **66 per mark** |
| 1409 | draft of the three ecclesiastical electors: a promise to hold 22 | (not kept) | — |
| **1417** | Rhenish treaty (without Cologne, with Jülich) | 20 Karat | «bei **altem Schrot (66 aus der Mark)**» |
| 1419 | renewed treaty, all four electors + Jülich | 19 Karat | — |
| **1490** | the last reduction | **18½ Karat** | — |

**Two figures survive this whole descent unchanged and both matter later.** The
**Schrot of 66 per Cologne mark** is named in 1386, again in 1402, and in 1417 is
already «*altes Schrot*» — the weight held while the fineness fell by a quarter.
And **23⅔ Karat**, the electors' figure before 1365, is exactly the fineness the
imperial ducat would carry from 1559: the Reich's high-fineness gold standard is
not an import but a restoration of its own oldest one.

Assays record the collapse between the treaty years: 1398 gives 22 Karat 8 Grän,
1400 gives 22 Karat 2 Grän and 20 Karat 10 Grän, 1401 gives 21, and Trier's
coins in 1408 assay at only 20-21½ against a treaty figure of 22½. By the
sixteenth century «Goldgulden» named a size rather than a standard: across
seventy catalogued types of 1436-1558 the rough weight holds at 3,17-3,28 g while
published finenesses run from .767 to .986 (`ducat_origins.md` §1).

## 2. The ducat as a struck coin before it was law, 1497-1558

German territories struck ducats for six decades before any imperial act
mentioned one. First strikes, given here only to fix the dates:

| year | issuer | denomination | rough |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1497** | **Hamburg** | 1 Ducat (Fr# 1100) | 3,46 g |
| 1513 | Württemberg, Ulrich | 2 Ducats | 7,03 g |
| 1531 | Aachen | 1½ and 4 Ducats, Ferdinand I's coronation | 5,25 / 13,65 g |
| 1537 | Passau, Ernest of Bavaria · Württemberg, Ulrich | 1 Ducat | 3,42 / 3,50 g |
| 1542-43 | Kaufbeuren | 1 Ducat | 3,51 g |
| 1547 | Saxony (Albertine), siege of Leipzig | 1 Ducat | 3,56 / 3,36 g |
| 1551 | Magdeburg, siege | 2 and 6 Ducats | 6,45 / 17,5 g |

**These are not one standard.** Computed per rough Cologne mark they scatter from
65,69 to 68,53, and the two Aachen denominations of a single 1531 issue disagree
with each other by 2,5 % — specimen weights from mints each cutting to its own
practice. **No pre-1559 German ducat sits on 67,00**
(`ducat_origins.md` §6g). What 1559 standardised was a coin type in circulation,
not an existing figure.

## 3. The three Reichsmünzordnungen

| | | gold provision |
|---|---|---|
| **1st** | **Esslingen, 1524** | declares «*die kölnische Mark für das allgemeine deutsche Münzgewicht*» — **233,856 g** as the empire-wide coin weight. Nothing about a ducat. Its attempt to raise the fine content back to 22 Karat failed. |
| **2nd** | **Augsburg, 1551** | the Goldgulden at 72 Kreuzer. **The ducat is not mentioned.** |
| **3rd** | **Augsburg, 19 August 1559** | «*Neue Goldmünze wurde der **Dukat***» — admitted as an imperial gold coin beside the Rheinischer Goldgulden. |

**The 1559 ordinance is Ferdinand I's**, issued at the Augsburg Diet. He had been
King of Hungary and Bohemia since 1526/27 — master of Kremnitz and the other
Hungarian gold mints — and Emperor since 1556. He did not harmonise the two
standards he held: the Hungarian coinage continued at 69 per Buda mark and 23¾
Karat under his own name through 1564, some 2,31 % above the imperial figure he
had just set (`ducat_origins.md` §6f).

## 4. The standard as decreed in 1559

The ordinance states the ducat in two equivalent forms, and the second is
derived from the first:

> «*Für den Dukaten gilt **67 Dukaten auf die Mark Gold zu 23 Karat 8 Grän** oder
> **67,944 Dukaten auf die feine Mark Gold***»

| | |
|---|---|
| Schrot | **67 pieces per rough Cölln. Marck** of 233,856 g |
| Korn | **23 Karat 8 Grän = 23⅔ = 986 1/9 ‰** |
| per piece | **3,4904 g rough · 3,4419 g fine** (the ordinance's own «3,442 g Gold») |
| per fine mark | 67 67/71 = 67,944 pieces |
| tariff | **104 Kreuzer**; the Rheinischer Goldgulden alongside at 75 |

**Recomputed, and the ordinance's own numbers check out to the last digit.**
233,856 / 67 = **3,49039 g** rough; × 23⅔/24 = **3,44191 g** fine, against the
printed «3,442 g». And 233,856 / 3,44191 = **67,944**, against the printed
67 67/71 = 67,9437. The two forms are the same standard stated twice, with the
rough-mark form primary.

The fine-mark figure of sixty-seven and sixty-seven seventy-firsts is what a
clerk computes when he needs it, not what a mint is set to. **The rough-mark 67
at a stated Korn is the primary form**, and every later German act restates it in
that shape.

**On the tariff.** Ducat 104 against Goldgulden 75 is a ratio of 1,387; against
the ducat's 3,4419 g fine that implies a gulden holding ≈2,48 g fine, i.e. about
16,8 Karat at the unchanged 66-per-mark Schrot — two carats below the 18½ of
1490. By the time the Reich made the ducat its principal gold coin, the coin it
displaced had fallen further still.

## 5. The standard's persistence, 1559-1876

What is remarkable about the figure is that nothing moved it. The
Reichsmünzordnung of 1559 set 67 / 23⅔, and German gold accounting still quotes
that pair two centuries later — the Convention era restates it verbatim, «*67
Dukaten auf die Mark Gold zu 23 Karat 8 Grän … (wie 3. Reichsmünzordnung,
1559)*», while every silver standard around it was renegotiated repeatedly.

The end comes from outside the ducat's own line:

| year | instrument | effect |
|---|---|---|
| **4 Dec 1871** | **Reichsmünzgesetz** | the Goldmark supersedes the Dukatenfuß Reich-wide; end of the standard |
| 9 Jul 1873 | extension to silver | phases in the new currency |
| **1 Jan 1876** | | Mark and Vereinsthaler become sole legal tender — **demonetisation** |

The last German ducats are struck in the early 1870s.

## 6. The standard inside this project's own scope

Ninety-one coins on `reichsdukatenfuss` sit outside the Danish realm in
`data/v2/final/`. First strikes per issuer, given to date each entry into the
standard:

| issuer | enters | first strike on the standard | leaves |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Royal Holstein** | 1591 | 1 Ungersk Gylden and ½ Portugaløser, **Haderslev 1591-93**, under Queen Sophie's regency for Christian IV | 1802 (Altona) |
| **Gottorp duchy** | 1601 | 1 Ducat, Johann Adolf | 1711-12 |
| **Sonderburg duchy** | 1619 | 1 Goldgulden | 1624 |
| **Rantzau county** | 1655 | 1 Ducat, Christian zu Rantzau, **Rantzau mint** | 1689 (Barmstedt, Detlef) |
| **Norburg-Plön duchy** | 1676 | 3 and 4 Ducat | 1760 |
| **Glücksburg duchy** | 1716 | 1 Ducat, Philip Ernst | — (single type) |
| **Free city of Lübeck** | 1789 | 1 Ducat | 1801 |

Two observations follow, both about **the standard's reach rather than any
coin**. The imperial figure is adopted in these territories over two centuries —
Haderslev in the 1590s, Gottorp from 1601, the small Sonderburg lines from the
1610s-1670s, Lübeck only at the very end of the eighteenth century — which is
what one expects of a standard that is available rather than imposed. And
**Lübeck, the first German city to hold a gold privilege in 1340 (§1), appears in
our corpus on this standard only from 1789**; the gap is a gap in our own
coverage, not in Lübeck's coinage, and is recorded as such in §7.4.

## 7. Open questions

1. **The founding anchors in `data/shared/fuesse.yml::reichsdukatenfuss.events`
   are stale.** Both `first_adoption.anywhere` and `first_mint.anywhere` are
   **1513**, and both notes cite «*Galster c2g-89/90, reign-span 1513-1523*» as
   the earliest attested piece. Those two coins were re-dated to the Grevens
   Fejde of **1534-1536** on 2026-08-15 (`dk_dukat_portugaloeser_fod.md` §0.3,
   commit `2db681f`), so the anchors now rest on a reading the coin data no
   longer supports. Two further points bear on the same field: the earliest
   Danish piece on the standard is Galster 46 of **1531**, and the event's own
   `anywhere_label` reads «*Heiliges Römisches Reich + Königreich Dänemark und
   Norwegen*» — a scope in which the earliest attested strike is **Hamburg
   1497** (§2), not a Danish coin at all. **A curator decision, not a defect to
   fix unilaterally**: the events drive the rendered timeline.
2. **Hamburg 1497's fineness.** The earliest German ducat is published with a
   weight and no assay, so whether it is a 23⅔-Karat coin cannot be said. The
   single most valuable missing datum on the German side.
3. **The drafting record of the 1559 ordinance.** Whether 67 / 23⅔ was set with
   the Hungarian standard in view is unrecorded (`ducat_origins.md` §7.13).
4. **Lübeck's gold between 1341 and 1789.** The city holds the first German gold
   privilege and appears in our corpus only from 1789. What it struck in between,
   and on which standard, is not covered here; `docs/research/hamburg.md` and
   `altona.md` are the adjacent dossiers, and neither addresses it.
5. **Whether any German territory struck ducats before Hamburg 1497.** The
   89-type sweep behind §2 is one catalogue's coverage, not a corpus.
